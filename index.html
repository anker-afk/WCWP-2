The Number

Open Instagram. Search "protein." You'll find creators telling you to eat 1 gram per pound of bodyweight. For a 150-pound person, that's 150 grams a day. It sounds specific. It sounds scientific. It has become the default advice across fitness social media.

Here's what the research actually says.

The Recommended Dietary Allowance of 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day is the scientifically established baseline for healthy adults. Higher intake (1.2 to 1.6g/kg) can benefit people losing weight or older adults preserving muscle. But intake above 2.0g/kg shows little additional benefit and can strain the kidneys.

What the science says
30g per meal
That's the maximum amount your muscles can use for repair and growth in a single sitting. Eating 60g in one meal doesn't double the benefit. A chicken breast, a cup of Greek yogurt with nuts, or a can of tuna each get you there.

Sources: ScienceInsights, "How Much Protein Does Your Body Actually Need?" (2026); CBS News / Sara Moniuszko (2025)

So where did 150 grams come from? Not from a lab. From a feed.

The Market

Protein is now a $114 billion industry. The number of high-protein shakes and drinks on the market rose 122% between 2020 and 2024. Chains like Starbucks and Dutch Bros have added protein options. PepsiCo's CEO told investors that "consumers are adopting protein solutions in the diet at a pace that was not the case a few years back."

The pipeline works in three steps.

Who creates the demand?
Fitness creators post "what I eat in a day" content showing 150 to 200 grams of protein, making it look like the standard. Viewers start thinking their 60 grams is not enough.
Who fulfills it?
The same creators partner with supplement brands. Discount codes, "link in bio," paid partnerships. The advice and the ad become indistinguishable.
How does the cycle sustain itself?
Users buy supplements, follow more creators, see more content telling them they need more protein. The cycle reinforces itself. The industry grows. The user's grocery bill grows with it.

As Joan Salge Blake, a clinical professor of nutrition at Boston University, puts it: "It's always something. First it was calories: everyone was counting calories. Then it was low-carb, then no added sugar. There's always a trend."

But this trend has a $114 billion industry behind it.

The Stakes

This is not just about money. It's about what happens to your body when one nutrient becomes an obsession and everything else gets displaced.

Lead in your powder

Consumer Reports tested 23 protein powders and shakes. More than two-thirds contained unsafe levels of lead. These are products marketed as health food.

Scurvy in 2025

Joan Salge Blake at BU describes cases of people on the carnivore diet, eating only meat for protein, developing scurvy because they dropped all fruits and vegetables. "Obviously that's extreme, but it does happen."

Supplements without blood work

Creatine, pre-workout, collagen, multivitamins, BCAAs. Many gym-goers take five or more supplements daily without a single blood test to know what they actually need. Registered dietitian Helen Tieu says: "For people who are able to reach their protein targets from whole foods, protein powders and supplements are not necessary."

What the industry measures is demand. What it does not measure is whether that demand reflects need.
